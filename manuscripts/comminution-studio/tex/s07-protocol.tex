\section{Experimental protocol}\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Cases and what each set of cases tests}\label{sec:protocol-cases}

Three sets of operating points serve three different questions, and no result of one set is read as evidence about another. The
seventeen circuit cases test the behaviour of the coupled engine: whether it reproduces the size-reduction ladder of a crushing circuit
and the trends of each lever, and whether its checks flag the controls. They are baked at their operating points and judged against the
numeric expected ranges each case declares (for example, for S01, a $P_{80}$ between $22$ and $35$~mm, a throughput between $520$ and
$780$~t/h and a reduction ratio between $3.5$ and $5.5$), ranges written from engineering relations and not from the engine's own value.
The ten HP500 surveys test prediction against measurement, for the two measured targets only, under the leave-one-survey-out protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:m-crushercal}. The two Latin-hypercube draws test the learned models against the engine. Each case also has two
variants computed live in the browser, a tight setting ($15\%$ lower) and an open setting ($15\%$ higher), which the studio shows and which
are not scored.

\subsection{Metrics}\label{sec:protocol-metrics}

For a target with measured values $y_i$ and held-out predictions $\hat y_i$, $i=1,\dots,N_s$, the point scores are
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAPE} = \frac{100}{N_s}\sum_{i=1}^{N_s}\left|\frac{\hat y_i - y_i}{y_i}\right|,\qquad
\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_s}\sum_{i=1}^{N_s}\left(\hat y_i - y_i\right)^2},\qquad
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2},
\label{eq:scores}
\end{equation}
and the largest deviation $\max_i|\hat y_i-y_i|/y_i$ in percent. A held-out $R^2$ can be negative: a predictor worse than the mean of the
measured values scores below zero. For an interval $[\ell_i,u_i]$ the coverage and the mean width are
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{cov} = \frac{1}{N_s}\sum_{i=1}^{N_s}\mathbb 1\!\left[\ell_i\le y_i\le u_i\right],\qquad
\bar w = \frac{1}{N_s}\sum_{i=1}^{N_s}(u_i-\ell_i),
\label{eq:coverage}
\end{equation}
and the mean log predictive density of a predictive distribution with density $p_i$ is $\overline{\mathrm{lpd}}=\frac{1}{N_s}\sum_i\log p_i(y_i)$.
For CrusherBayes $p_i$ is the posterior predictive density, estimated as the log of the mean over the retained samples of the Gaussian
predictive density at each sample; for a residual-band predictor it is the normal density with standard deviation $\sigma_{-i}$ at its
residual. The contraction of a parameter is the ratio of its posterior to its prior standard deviation on its natural scale; below $0.5$ the
surveys identify the parameter, and near one it stays at its prior. For the learned models, $R^2$ and MAPE are computed per output in the
original units over the $2196$ held-out points; the false-alarm rate is the share of held-out points that raise an alarm and the detection
rate the share of planted points that do.

\emph{Uncertainty of a held-out error.} Ten surveys give each MAPE a sampling error that is of the order of the differences between the
predictors, so every held-out MAPE of the surveys carries a nonparametric bootstrap interval over the surveys. With $a_{k,i}$ the absolute
percentage error of predictor $k$ at survey $i$ and $I^{(b)}$ the $b$-th of $B=\uqB$ resamples of the ten survey indices with replacement
(seed $\uqSeed$),
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAPE}^{(b)}_k = \frac{1}{N_s}\sum_{i\in I^{(b)}} a_{k,i},\qquad
\Delta^{(b)}_{k,l} = \mathrm{MAPE}^{(b)}_k - \mathrm{MAPE}^{(b)}_l ,
\label{eq:bootstrap}
\end{equation}
and the $95\%$ interval of a MAPE, or of the difference of two predictors, is the $2.5$th to the $97.5$th percentile of its $B$ values. The
same resampled surveys serve every predictor, so a difference is paired: a survey that is hard for both predictors moves both errors
together. A ranking of two predictors is stated only when the interval of their difference excludes zero; otherwise the two are reported as
not distinguishable on these surveys. The rule applies to every two predictors, not only to a predictor and the line or the backbone, so the
interval of every pair is computed on the same resamples (Table~\ref{tab:pairs}). The predictions are those of the committed documents (the strict CrusherCal predictions and the
CrusherBayes predictive means), except two that no document stores per survey: the constant mean of each fold, computed from the
measured values of its training surveys, and the label-shuffle control, recomputed with the product's own functions and seed on the
calibrated backbone of each fold at full precision. The computation is the report's script
\texttt{figures/\allowbreak{}heldout\_uq.py}, which recomputes every committed MAPE from these predictions before it resamples.

\subsection{Baselines and controls}\label{sec:protocol-baselines}

Every comparison has a baseline that a method must beat before it claims skill. On the surveys: the constant mean of the training surveys
(no information), the label-shuffle control (a fit that learns noise, which must not beat the backbone), and the setting-only line (one
feature, no physics), which is the bar for any claim on power. For the surrogate the baseline is the engine itself, which is what the slot
answers with when its bundle is invalid; for the autoencoder the baseline is the envelope check. The leaky variant of every survey fit is
reported beside the strict one, so that the optimism of letting a survey into its own fit is measured and not assumed. The published
calibration enters twice and is run, never quoted: its classification, $t_{10}$ and power constants are run in the engine, with the engine's
scalars refit per fold, and its separate throughput model (Equation~\eqref{eq:rocha}) is run on the ten surveys as a throughput predictor,
with its fitted factor as published and refit per fold. A predictor whose difference to the setting-only line has an interval that includes
zero has no claim of skill over the line on that target.

\subsection{The protocol shared by every survey predictor}\label{sec:protocol-shared}

All predictors of the surveys use the same ten folds, the same targets in the same units, the same score functions and the same nested band
construction (Section~\ref{sec:m-crushercal}); CrusherBayes uses its posterior predictive interval in place of the band. In every fold every
fitted quantity, the capacity base, the power-form constants, the ridge and free-form coefficients, the line, $M_{ic}$ and the no-load power,
and the whole CrusherBayes posterior, is refit on the nine training surveys, and the held-out survey enters only the prediction. A test plants
an absurd target in one survey and checks that every other fold's prediction moves and the planted survey's own does not. With $N_s=10$ the
protocol has a resolution of $0.1$ in coverage, and the uncertainty of the errors is the bootstrap of Section~\ref{sec:protocol-metrics}. The
adoption rule of CrusherBayes uses a margin of three points of MAPE, fixed before the run as a rule; it is not a measured noise level, and the
bootstrap intervals of the differences between the predictors are wider (Section~\ref{sec:results-uq}). No predictor is tuned on the held-out scores: every constant of every fit was set before the first run and is
recorded in its document.

\subsection{Seeds, repetitions, hardware and run times}\label{sec:protocol-seeds}

The survey evaluation is deterministic given the seed $42$, which fixes the label-shuffle permutation (one per target, shared by the strict
and the leaky folds); CrusherBayes spawns one seed per
posterior from a seed sequence started at $42$, uses that seed plus one for the predictive draws of the posterior, and its document is byte-identical whatever the number of worker
processes. The learned models use the training seed $12345$ and the held-out seed $67890$, with torch single-threaded. The
particle-replacement check of Section~\ref{sec:validation} uses five seeds per parcel count. Each posterior is one chain of twenty walkers;
its repetition is the eleven posteriors of the folds, whose convergence diagnostics are reported. The bootstrap of the held-out errors uses the seed $\uqSeed$. The measured run times, recorded on \resDate{} on a
Windows 11 workstation with Python $\resPython$ and Node $24$, are in Table~\ref{tab:resources}: about $\resBakeMin$~minutes for the whole bake,
of which about $\resHpMin$ are the HP500 step with the CrusherBayes sampler ($140{,}000$ log-probability evaluations per posterior, eleven
posteriors), $\resModelsS$~s to train and evaluate both learned models on the processor, and a median of $\resEngineMed$~ms per engine
evaluation. No stage uses a graphics processor.

\subsection{Acceptance criteria}\label{sec:protocol-acceptance}

Table~\ref{tab:acceptance} states, for each method, the criterion that a test checks and that would fail the build. They are criteria of
correctness and of reporting, not of accuracy against the plant: a method that predicts the surveys poorly passes when its result is computed
as stated and reported as it is, and its poor accuracy is a result (Section~\ref{sec:results}). The distinction matters for the power
results: the criterion of the Bond power and of Morrell's energy is that their held-out numbers are computed and reported as they come, so the
build passes with a held-out power error of $25.94\%$ for the backbone and $38.08\%$ for Morrell through the origin. Each criterion names its
test in the product's design document, and a requirement without a test that exists fails a check of the repository; the criteria below are
therefore the ones the committed build satisfies, not intentions.
